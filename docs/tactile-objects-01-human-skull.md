# Tactile object 01 — the human skull

**ChipuRobo tactile objects and 3D printing · first iteration**
*A field reference for school visits, and a record for whoever designs the next one.*

---

This document does two jobs, and it is worth being honest about both.

The first is immediate: it is what a facilitator reads the night before a school
visit and skims on the day. That is §3 onwards.

The second is longer-term. This is the **first** tactile object we are putting
in front of learners, and everything we find out on these visits decides what
objects two, three and four look like. §7 is the part that makes the next one
better, and it is the part most likely to be skipped. Please do not skip it.

---

## 1. Why a skull first

The choice was not arbitrary, and the reasoning generalises — these are the
properties to look for in the next object too.

- **Every learner already owns one.** A learner can check the model against
  their own head with their hands. Almost no other teaching object gives you a
  free reference copy in every pair of hands in the room.
- **It survives printing.** Big, closed, mostly convex forms. No thin
  spans, no moving parts, no assembly.
- **The landmarks are unmistakable by touch.** Eye sockets, teeth, the jaw,
  the seams across the top. A blind learner can locate themselves on this
  object within seconds, which is not true of, say, a printed map or circuit.
- **It is bilaterally symmetric.** A learner who finds a feature on one side can
  predict the other. That builds confidence fast.
- **It rewards turning it over.** The most interesting feature — the hole where
  the spinal cord enters — is underneath, so the object teaches the habit of
  exploring all of a thing rather than its front.

**Weakness to carry forward:** it is a single static object. Nothing moves
except the jaw, and often not even that. A future object that hinges, separates
or assembles would teach something this one cannot.

---

## 2. Before you start

**Hand it round, early and for a long time.** The whole advantage over a poster
is that it can be picked up. Plan for the model to be out of your hands for most
of the session.

**Say what it is and is not.** A printed copy of an adult human skull, at or
near life size. Not a real skull, not anyone's skull. Say it plainly and early —
some learners are uneasy, and naming that settles a room much faster than
ignoring it.

**Orient before you name.** Front, back, top, underneath. Nobody can hold a term
before they know where they are looking.

**Six words is a good session.** A class that leaves knowing six terms properly
has done better than one that heard forty. The terms marked **core** below are
the six.

---

## 3. The two halves

Teach this first; everything else hangs off it.

| Term | Say it | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **Cranium** *(core)* | KRAY-nee-um | The braincase — the domed part around the brain. |
| **Facial skeleton** | — | The face: eye sockets, nose opening, cheeks, jaws, teeth. |
| **Calvaria** | kal-VAIR-ee-uh | The skullcap — just the domed roof. |
| **Cranial cavity** | — | The hollow space inside where the brain sits. |

> **Ask:** *"Which part is doing a job of protecting, and which part is doing a
> job of eating, seeing and breathing?"* Learners get there themselves.

---

## 4. The bones

### The cranium — eight bones

| Bone | Say it | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Frontal** *(core)* | FRUN-tal | Forehead, and the upper rim of both eye sockets | One. Learners can feel it on themselves. |
| **Parietal** | pa-RYE-uh-tal | The two big plates over the top and upper sides | **Two**, meeting along the midline. |
| **Temporal** | TEM-por-al | The side, around and behind the ear | **Two.** Contains the ear structures. |
| **Occipital** | ok-SIP-it-al | Back and base | One. Has the big hole underneath. |
| **Sphenoid** | SFEE-noyd | Deep inside; a wing shows at the temple | One. Butterfly-shaped. |
| **Ethmoid** | ETH-moyd | Deep behind the nose, between the eye sockets | One. |

Total: frontal 1 + parietal 2 + temporal 2 + occipital 1 + sphenoid 1 + ethmoid 1 = **8**.

> **Be honest about the last two.** On most printed skulls the sphenoid and
> ethmoid are fused into the print or too enclosed to see. Name them, say you
> cannot show them properly on this model, and move on. Pointing vaguely at
> something you cannot actually show teaches learners that anatomy is arbitrary.

### The face — fourteen bones, five worth naming

| Bone | Say it | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Mandible** *(core)* | MAN-dib-ul | The lower jaw. The only part of the skull that moves. |
| **Maxilla** *(core)* | mak-SILL-uh | Upper jaw, holding the upper teeth. Two, fused at the midline. |
| **Zygomatic** | zy-go-MAT-ik | The cheekbone. Two. |
| **Nasal** | NAY-zal | Bridge of the nose. Two small bones. |
| **Palatine** | PAL-uh-tyne | Back of the roof of the mouth. Two — look underneath. |

The rest — lacrimal, inferior nasal concha, vomer — are small, internal and
rarely resolvable on a print. Mention only if asked.

> **Ask:** *"Only one bone in the whole head moves. Which one, and how do you
> know?"* Everyone chews the answer out in seconds.

## 5. Landmarks, holes and seams

**A landmark is a shape on a bone, not a bone of its own.** Make this explicit —
it is the most common confusion.

| Feature | Say it | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **Orbit** *(core)* | OR-bit | The eye socket. Formed by several bones meeting, not one. |
| **Nasal aperture** | — | The pear-shaped nose opening. The nose itself is cartilage, so it is missing. |
| **Zygomatic arch** | — | The bony bridge from cheek to just in front of the ear. |
| **Mastoid process** | MAS-toyd | The lump behind the earlobe. Part of the temporal bone. |
| **Styloid process** | STY-loyd | A thin spike below the ear. **Often broken or absent on prints.** |
| **Alveolar process** | al-VEE-o-lar | The ridge holding the tooth sockets. |
| **Hard palate** | — | The bony roof of the mouth. Underneath. |

| Opening | Say it | What passes through |
|---|---|---|
| **Foramen magnum** *(core)* | for-AY-men MAG-num | The large hole underneath — the spinal cord enters here. Latin for "great hole"; the name does exactly what it says. |
| **Mental foramen** | — | Small hole either side of the front of the jaw. Nerves to the lower lip. |
| **Auditory canal** | AW-dit-or-ee | The ear opening, in the temporal bone. |

| Seam / joint | Say it | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **Suture** *(core)* | SOO-cher | An immovable joint where two skull bones meet. They look like cracks, and learners **will** ask if the model is broken. Answer before it is asked. |
| **Coronal suture** | ko-ROH-nal | Across the top: frontal against both parietals. |
| **Sagittal suture** | SAJ-it-al | Front to back along the midline, between the parietals. |
| **Lambdoid suture** | LAM-doyd | Across the back: parietals against occipital. |
| **TMJ** | tem-por-oh-man-DIB-yoo-lar | The jaw joint, in front of the ear. The only freely movable joint here. |

> **Ask:** *"Turn it over. What is that big hole for?"* This is usually the
> moment the object stops being a thing and starts being a body.

> **Ask:** *"If the seams are joints, why can't you move them?"* Opens the
> difference between a joint that moves and one that locks.

**Fontanelles** (FON-ta-nels) are the soft gaps in an infant skull that let the
head compress during birth and the brain grow after. They close within about two
years. **An adult model cannot show them** — if you raise it, be clear you are
describing something absent from the object in their hands.

### Teeth

An adult has **32**: 16 upper, 16 lower. Per side, top and bottom — **2 incisors
(cutting), 1 canine (tearing), 2 premolars (crushing), 3 molars (grinding)**.

Printed models often have teeth missing or fused into a single ridge. Check
yours before the session and say what you have.

## 6. Tactile delivery — the actual point of this object

This is not an accessibility footnote. It is the reason a printed skull is worth
more to us than a poster, and it should sit at the centre of the session plan.

- **For a blind learner, the model is not an illustration of the lesson — it is
  the lesson.** Give it to them first and for longest, not last and briefly.
- **Guide by landmark, never by pointing.** *"Find the two ridges above the eye
  sockets, then run your thumb straight back over the top"* works. *"It's here"*
  does not.
- **Sequence the touch.** Whole shape → front → back → underneath → inside.
  Jumping between regions is disorienting and learners lose the map.
- **Build the bone names on the sutures.** They are the best tactile feature on
  the whole object — clearly felt, unmistakable, and they divide the bones from
  each other physically. This is a general principle worth carrying to object
  two: *find the feature that segments the object by touch, and teach the parts
  off it.*
- **Warn before the foramen magnum.** Putting a finger unexpectedly into a hole
  in a skull is startling.
- **Two copies beat one.** Learners sharing a single model spend the session
  waiting. If the print budget allows one improvement, make it a second copy.

For learners who are deaf or hard of hearing, agree signs for the six core terms
with the KSL interpreter **before** the session. Several of these words have no
settled sign and get fingerspelled, which is slow and easy to lose. Fixing six
terms in advance makes that manageable.

**Record it in the dashboard.** The session's adaptations — braille and tactile
materials, KSL interpretation — are a fixed list against each session. Recording
that this object was used is what turns "we make tactile materials" from a claim
into a number we can report.

## 7. What to bring back from the visit

This is the first iteration. The next object is designed from what happens on
these visits, so it matters that someone actually writes this down. Five minutes
afterwards, while it is fresh.

**About the object**

- What broke, and where? (Expect the styloid process and the teeth.)
- Was the size right in a learner's hands — too big, too small?
- Did the sutures read as real separations by touch, or as decorative grooves?
- Did the jaw move? Did learners expect it to?
- How many learners could realistically share one copy?

**About the session**

- Which terms stuck without effort, and which needed repeating?
- What did learners ask that this document does not answer?
- What did a blind or low-vision learner find first, unprompted? *That feature
  is the one to design around next time.*
- Where did the session stall?

**About the next object**

- What did learners want to see that a skull cannot show? (Movement, layers,
  scale, something that comes apart.)
- Did anything about the object mislead them?

## 8. What this model cannot show

Say these out loud during the session. Learners trust a facilitator who names
the limits of their own equipment.

- **No cartilage** — the nose and ears are missing entirely, which is why the
  face looks wrong. Genuinely good discussion.
- **Internal detail is largely lost** — sphenoid, ethmoid, sinuses, the fine
  structure of the skull base.
- **Fine spikes break**, the styloid process especially.
- **Sutures may be decorative** — the model may be one solid piece with grooves
  rather than real separations. Check yours.
- **It is one adult skull.** Real skulls vary a lot between people. Do not let
  learners conclude every skull is exactly this.

---

## 9. Six words to leave behind

If the session is cut short or the class is very young, aim for these and
nothing else:

1. **Cranium** — the braincase
2. **Mandible** — the lower jaw, the only part that moves
3. **Maxilla** — the upper jaw
4. **Orbit** — the eye socket
5. **Suture** — the seams that lock, not break
6. **Foramen magnum** — where the spinal cord comes in

## Notes for whoever maintains this

Three things are deliberately missing, because inventing them would make this
document look more authoritative than it is:

- **CBC strand mapping.** This should name the Integrated Science strand and
  sub-strand it supports, but that needs someone holding the current syllabus.
  Guessed strand codes are worse than none.
- **Kiswahili terms.** `Fuvu la kichwa` (skull) and `taya` (jaw) are safe, but a
  full parallel glossary needs someone who teaches science in Kiswahili — not a
  translation attempt.
- **Print specification.** Filament, layer height, infill, scale and whether the
  calvaria and mandible print separately are not recorded here because they
  should come from the machine and file we actually use, not from a general
  recommendation. Fill this in from the first successful print and this document
  becomes reproducible.

Check §5 and §8 against the specific model you are carrying before each visit.
Print quality varies more than a document can predict.
